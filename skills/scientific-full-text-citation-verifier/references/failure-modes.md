# Failure modes and falsification checklist

Use this checklist for each promising candidate and once more across the final answer.

## Retrieval and provenance failures

- Treating a title, keyword match, snippet, related-paper recommendation, or abstract similarity as support.
- Claiming full-text inspection after viewing only metadata, an abstract, selected preview, or incomplete extraction.
- Citing a review's assertion as primary evidence without checking the cited study.
- Copying an identifier, quotation, or evidence location from an unreliable secondary page without verification.
- Mixing evidence from one version with metadata or pagination from another.
- Missing a correction, expression of concern, retraction, or materially changed version.

## Claim-matching failures

- Same topic but wrong entity, isoform, biomarker, drug, pathway, or disease subtype.
- Opposite or non-comparable direction.
- Wrong tissue, cell type, species, demographic, disease stage, treatment state, or recurrence status.
- Different endpoint, surrogate, time horizon, comparator, dose, or exposure.
- Gene-expression evidence used for protein abundance, localization, function, or activity.
- Group-level association used for individual prediction, diagnosis, or prognosis.
- A null or exploratory subgroup result represented as a general effect.

## Design and inference failures

- Association represented as causation or regulation.
- Cross-sectional evidence represented as temporal progression.
- Bioinformatic prediction represented as experimental validation.
- Docking or in-silico affinity represented as biological or therapeutic efficacy.
- In-vitro or animal evidence represented as a confirmed human effect.
- Retrospective association represented as prospective clinical utility.
- Mechanistic language without perturbation or mechanism-relevant evidence.
- Statistical significance represented as clinical importance.
- Absence of evidence represented as evidence of absence.
- Small, biased, underpowered, or exploratory evidence given definitive wording.

## Citation-chain failures

- The cited source does not contain the assertion attributed to it.
- The original study measured a proxy rather than the claimed endpoint.
- A review compresses several weaker studies into stronger wording.
- A citing paper repeats an error or overstatement from an earlier source.
- The chain ends at a hypothesis, narrative statement, or inaccessible source rather than primary evidence.

## Reporting failures

- Hiding rejected candidates or omitting the exact reason for rejection.
- Assigning a high score because a paper is prestigious, recent, highly cited, or topically close.
- Using a numeric score without explaining content correspondence and deductions.
- Averaging conflicting studies into a single confident conclusion.
- Omitting access status, source version, or material limitations.
- Rewriting the user's claim silently to make a candidate appear suitable.

## Final falsification questions

For each recommended citation answer:

1. What exact result supports the claim?
2. Is that result generated by this study or merely cited background?
3. Did the study measure the asserted entity, direction, context, and outcome?
4. Does its design justify the claim's causal or mechanistic strength?
5. What is the strongest reason to reject it, and why does it survive or fail?
6. Could a reader locate the evidence from the reported section/figure/table/page?
7. Would the citation remain defensible if the title and abstract were hidden?

Reject or qualify the paper whenever these questions expose a material gap.
